# Histoire de la folie à l'âge classique

**Referência ABNT completa:**
FOUCAULT, Michel. *Histoire de la folie à l'âge classique*. Paris: Plon, 1961. 656 p.

**Tradução para o inglês:**
FOUCAULT, Michel. *Madness and Civilization: A History of Insanity in the Age of Reason*. Translated by Richard Howard. New York: Pantheon Books, 1965.

## Resumo

Foucault's landmark study traces the historical transformation of madness from the Renaissance to the Classical age. Rather than viewing the history of psychiatry as a progress toward enlightenment, Foucault demonstrates how the "discovery" of mental illness in the eighteenth century involved a rupture in Western thought, marking a shift from a Renaissance understanding of madness as part of the human condition to a Classical understanding of it as pathological otherness.

In the Renaissance, Foucault argues, madness occupied a liminal space within culture—represented in literature and art, feared but integrated into social imagination. The mad wandered freely (exemplified by *Ship of Fools* imagery) or were confined alongside the poor and sick in mixed institutions. With the Great Confinement of the Classical period (roughly 1650-1800), madness became isolated, enclosed behind walls, and increasingly medicalized.

The book's central claim challenges psychiatric orthodoxy: the asylum was not built to cure the mad but to hide them away alongside other "undesirables." Moral treatment and therapeutic optimism came only later and represented a secondary justification for confinement that had already occurred for economic and social reasons. Foucault thus inverts the teleological narrative of psychiatric progress, suggesting instead that modernity created the very category of "mental illness" as an instrument of social discipline.

The study extends beyond institutional history to explore the philosophical and artistic dimensions of madness. Through analysis of literature, painting, and medical texts, Foucault shows how madness became silenced—stripped of the transgressive voice it possessed in Renaissance culture and relegated to the status of mere pathology to be observed, categorized, and treated by medical science.
