# Histoire de la sexualité, Tome 1: La Volonté de savoir

**Referência ABNT completa:**
FOUCAULT, Michel. *Histoire de la sexualité, Tome 1: La Volonté de savoir*. Paris: Gallimard, 1976. 211 p.

**English translation:**
FOUCAULT, Michel. *The History of Sexuality, Volume 1: An Introduction*. Translated by Robert Hurley. New York: Pantheon Books, 1978.

## Resumo

*La Volonté de savoir* inaugurates Foucault's genealogical investigation of how sexuality emerged as a historical category deeply entangled with power, knowledge, and truth. Contrary to common belief in sexual repression, Foucault argues that modern sexuality is constituted through proliferation of discourse about sex—a "will to knowledge" (volonté de savoir) that simultaneously produces sexuality as an object and masks the operation of power.

The book challenges the "repressive hypothesis"—the assumption that Victorian society repressed sexuality and that liberation requires speaking about sex. Foucault demonstrates instead that Western societies have been obsessed with talking about sex: confession in religious contexts, medical investigation of sexuality, psychoanalytic discourse all represent ways of producing sexuality as an object of knowledge and power. The effect is not less power over sexuality but greater, more refined power operating through knowledge production rather than prohibition.

Foucault traces how sexuality became a focus of power through medical and scientific discourse beginning in the nineteenth century. Nineteenth-century physicians, psychiatrists, and sexologists invented categories of sexual perversion, identifying homosexuality, masturbation, and other practices as pathologies requiring investigation and correction. This medicalization produced sexuality as a scientific object while simultaneously establishing norms regarding proper sexuality. Power operates not through silence but through proliferation of utterances about sex.

Crucially, Foucault identifies sexuality as produced through power-knowledge: knowledge about sexuality is simultaneously a technique of power for governing populations and individuals. Sexuality becomes biopolitical—a target of public health concerns, demographic policy, and moral regulation. Yet Foucault also identifies the possibility of counter-conduct: resistance to sexual normalization through affirmation of alternative sexualities and rejection of the psychiatric categories medicalizing non-normative desire.
