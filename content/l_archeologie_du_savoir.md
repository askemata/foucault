# L'Archéologie du savoir

**Referência ABNT completa:**
FOUCAULT, Michel. *L'Archéologie du savoir*. Paris: Gallimard, 1969. 275 p.

**English translation:**
FOUCAULT, Michel. *The Archaeology of Knowledge*. Translated by A. M. Sheridan Smith. New York: Pantheon Books, 1972.

## Resumo

*L'Archéologie du savoir* is Foucault's most explicit methodological treatise, formalizing the archaeological approach first demonstrated in earlier works. Rather than a history of ideas or intellectual progress, archaeology excavates the underlying rules, discontinuities, and structures that enable certain statements to be made as true (or false) within particular historical periods. The book responds to critics and consolidates Foucault's approach for investigating how knowledge is historically constituted.

Foucault introduces key archaeological concepts: the *statement* (énoncé) as the basic unit of analysis rather than ideas or authors; the *archive* as the ensemble of rules governing what can be said in a given period; *discourse* as systems of statements organized by regularities rather than individual intent. Rather than asking "what does a thinker really mean?", archaeology asks "what rules make this statement possible? What preconditions allow these particular formulations to appear as meaningful and true?"

The work rejects teleology—the notion that knowledge progresses toward truth. Instead, archaeology shows how different historical periods operate according to different rules of formation. A statement is true or false not according to correspondence with reality but according to the discursive rules constituting that historical moment. This does not mean truth is merely subjective; rather, truth is produced within historically specific systems governed by rules we can analyze.

Foucault also addresses how knowledge relates to power. Discourse is not simply representation; discourses are "practices that systematically form the objects of which they speak." What can be said, what counts as knowledge, and who is authorized to speak are all regulated by power. Archaeology thus reveals how knowledge and power are inseparable—the production of knowledge always involves power relations, and power always operates through knowledge.
