# Les Mots et les Choses: Une archéologie des sciences humaines

**Referência ABNT completa:**

**English translation:**
FOUCAULT, Michel. *The Order of Things: An Archaeology of the Human Sciences*. Translated by Alan Sheridan. New York: Pantheon Books, 1970.

## Resumo

*Les Mots et les Choses* is arguably Foucault's most influential work, presenting his theory of *épistémé*—the underlying structures of knowledge that make possible particular ways of organizing and understanding the world in any given historical period. Rather than tracing a continuous development of knowledge toward objective truth, Foucault identifies discontinuous ruptures between distinct epistemic regimes, each governed by its own rules and possibilities.

Foucault identifies three major epistémés in Western thought. The Renaissance epistémé (16th century) was structured by resemblance: knowledge sought similarity and analogy between things. The Classical epistémé (17th-18th centuries) replaced resemblance with representation and order: knowledge became representation of the world in language and numbers, exemplified by the analytical method and the birth of taxonomy. The modern epistémé (19th century onward) introduced *man* as both subject and object of knowledge, giving rise to the human sciences—anthropology, psychology, sociology, linguistics.

The book's famous opening analysis of Diego Velázquez's painting *Las Meninas* serves as a methodological model, demonstrating how representation structures visibility and the conditions under which knowledge becomes possible. Foucault argues that the human sciences emerged from a peculiar configuration of nineteenth-century thought and do not represent timeless universal knowledge. Rather, they are contingent historical products that may dissolve as new epistémés emerge.

The work also introduces Foucault's archaeological method: rather than seek origins or track influence, archaeology excavates the rules and structures that enable certain statements to be made and others to be excluded. This approach challenges both traditional history of ideas and structuralist interpretation, opening new possibilities for understanding how knowledge is historically contingent yet remarkably stable within particular periods.
