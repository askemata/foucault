# Naissance de la clinique: Une archéologie du regard médical

**Referência ABNT completa:**
FOUCAULT, Michel. *Naissance de la clinique: Une archéologie du regard médical*. Paris: Presses Universitaires de France, 1963. 227 p.

**Tradução para o inglês:**
FOUCAULT, Michel. *The Birth of the Clinic: An Archaeology of Medical Perception*. Translated by A. M. Sheridan Smith. New York: Pantheon Books, 1973.

## Resumo

*Naissance de la clinique* traces the historical emergence of modern clinical medicine from the late eighteenth century, showing how medicine transformed from a classificatory science organized by abstract nosology into an empirical discipline centered on direct observation of individual patients' bodies. Foucault identifies this shift not as scientific progress toward truth but as a fundamental reorganization of the medical gaze—a new way of seeing, speaking, and knowing disease.

Before the clinical revolution, medicine organized disease into abstract categories independent of individual patients. A disease had a name, a place in a classification system, but not necessarily a body. With the clinic's emergence—particularly after the French Revolution's opening of hospitals to clinical teaching—disease became localized in bodies. The living patient became the primary object of medical attention, requiring a new language (symptomatology) and new methods (autopsy, anatomical dissection) to correlate observable signs with internal pathology.

Foucault demonstrates this transformation through careful analysis of medical texts, particularly comparing pre-clinical nosology with the emergence of anatomical pathology. The clinical method assumed that disease manifests itself in bodily signs readable through systematic observation. This seemingly obvious procedure actually represents a profound historical shift: the construction of "the patient" as a knowable object and of medical perception as a disciplined, teachable practice.

Importantly, Foucault argues that this transformation in medicine parallels broader changes in institutional organization and social structure. The clinic emerged alongside other disciplinary institutions and new forms of power targeting populations. Medical knowledge and modern biopolitics develop together, making medicine integral to how modern societies manage and regulate populations.
