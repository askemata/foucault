# Surveiller et punir: Naissance de la prison

**Referência ABNT completa:**
FOUCAULT, Michel. *Surveiller et punir: Naissance de la prison*. Paris: Gallimard, 1975. 318 p.

**English translation:**
FOUCAULT, Michel. *Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the Prison*. Translated by Alan Sheridan. New York: Pantheon Books, 1977.

## Resumo

*Surveiller et punir* is Foucault's landmark analysis of how modern societies shift from spectacle-based public punishment to internalized self-regulation through discipline. Rather than tracing the humanization of punishment, Foucault argues that disciplinary techniques represent a more insidious form of power—one that operates through observation, normalization, and the production of docile, productive subjects.

Foucault opens with a striking contrast: the spectacled execution of Damiens in 1757 and the prison timetable of 1837. This shift marks not progress but transformation in power's modality. Public spectacle inscribed power on the body through visible suffering; discipline operates invisibly through spatial organization, timetables, hierarchical observation, and normalizing judgment. The prison paradigmatically embodies these techniques but is not unique to prisons—schools, hospitals, factories, and military barracks all employ disciplinary methods.

Central to Foucault's analysis is the Panopticon, Jeremy Bentham's architectural design where a central tower allows surveillance of all prisoners while they cannot know if they are being watched. This structure internalizes surveillance; prisoners behave as if always observed, developing self-regulation. Discipline succeeds not through constant observation but through the possibility of observation, producing consciousness of visibility. More broadly, modernity saturates with such observational architecture: city planning, workplace organization, and hierarchical bureaucracies make visibility and normalization constant conditions.

Discipline works through three mechanisms: hierarchical observation (seeing without being seen), normalizing judgment (establishing standards against which to measure), and the examination (combining observation and judgment to produce knowledge and individuality). The result is docile bodies optimized for productivity and social order. Yet Foucault also notes points of resistance: prison rebellions, escapes, and refusals show that discipline never achieves total control. Power relations always contain possibility of counter-conduct.
